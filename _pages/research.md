Synthetic polymers are produced at enormous scale, and a growing share is designed to be biodegradable. Whether a material actually degrades in the environment, and how quickly, depends on its chemical structure, the material's form, and the conditions it meets. My research quantifies these processes with analytical tools that can follow polymer carbon directly.

Biodegradation of polymers in soils
------
Polymers such as biodegradable mulch films are intentionally left in agricultural soils, but proving that they are fully broken down is analytically difficult. Using polymers made from <sup>13</sup>C-labelled monomers, I track how polymer carbon is converted into CO<sub>2</sub> and incorporated into microbial biomass, and I have developed methods to quantify polyester residues extracted from soil. This work shows how chemical structure, including the choice of aromatic and aliphatic building blocks, controls biodegradation rates in soil.

Photochemical fate of plastics in the ocean
------
At Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution I studied how sunlight degrades consumer plastics at the sea surface, and how formulation choices (additives such as titanium dioxide) and biofouling change that fate. This work continues through a U.S. National Science Foundation project on the controls of plastic photochemical reactivity in the surface ocean. It also led to new analytical tools, such as diffusion-ordered NMR spectroscopy for rapid determination of plastic molecular weight.

Degradable and recyclable polyethylene-like materials
------
Together with polymer chemists at the University of Konstanz, I evaluate new polyethylene-like polyesters made from bio- and waste-sourceable monomers. These materials combine the useful properties of polyethylene with chemical recyclability and biodegradability. With isotope labelling, enzymatic assays, and solid-state NMR, we link crystalline structure and chain composition to how readily these materials break down.
